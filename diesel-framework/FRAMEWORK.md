# Australia Diesel Import Reduction Framework

Oct 4, 2026 · @Andrew Baker

Australia can roughly halve net diesel imports by 2035, from about 29 GL to 15.5 GL a year, and approach zero by about 2045. Getting there means electrifying mining, heavy trucks and remote power first, with domestic renewable diesel and halophytes as a supplement. The first 12 months must protect food supply, because the country holds about 32 days of diesel while a US export ban is being openly discussed.

- **The exposure.** Australia imports about 87% of its 33 GL of diesel, holds a third of the IEA's 90-day requirement, and buys about 10% of the world's seaborne diesel.
- **Where the diesel goes.** Mining and trucks use half, light vehicles a quarter, and agriculture about 7%.
- **What works fastest.** Remote solar-battery hybrids already cost less than diesel power. Electric trucks pay back in 2 to 4 years on the Hume. Truck rebates avoid imports at about A$0.24 a litre, against about A$1.00 for ute grants.
- **Battery swapping.** Hubs that offer both swapping and megawatt charging, beside solar and wind farms on the Hume, Newell, Pacific and Bruce corridors. For harvest machinery, shared co-op swap stations, sized at about 20,000 ha of cropping.
- **Halophytes.** Food-neutral but small: about 1 to 3% of demand within a decade at realistic yields. Canola that is currently exported could supply about 7% sooner.
- **Corrections to earlier work.** The halophyte reports overstated diesel-days about threefold, and the farm swap station's diesel savings about a hundredfold. Both are corrected below.
- **Cost.** About A$12 bn to 15 bn of public money over 10 years, roughly 18 months of fuel tax credits, to save A$17 bn to 28 bn a year in imports.

## Planning scenario

The framework plans for a prolonged refined-product shock: Hormuz contested, the Red Sea threatened, Russian diesel off the market, and a possible US export ban. Public reporting to 3 October 2026 supports almost all of this, with two nuances: Hormuz crude flows have largely recovered while diesel flows have not, and the US ban has been floated but not enacted.

**Response already used.** In March the government released about 762 ML of Minimum Stockholding Obligation stock (a 20% cut), relaxed diesel flashpoint and sulphur standards, and appointed a national fuel coordinator ([SBS](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/australia-fuel-shortage-2026/zl0grg7ey)). Its modelling triggers voluntary demand cuts at 15 days of diesel cover and rationing at 10 days ([ABC, 31 Mar](https://www.abc.net.au/news/2026-03-31/modelling-assumes-rationing-if-diesel-stockpile-drops-to-10-days/106513966)). The fuel task force has since been disbanded. Whether the stockholding relief was extended past 30 September is unconfirmed.

**Why Australia is uniquely exposed.** Australia buys about 10% of the world's seaborne diesel, so it competes with every other importer when supply tightens. About 10% of global refining capacity is offline across the Middle East and Russia, and US retail diesel hit a record US$6.53/gal on 22 Sep ([Daily Caller, 23 Sep](https://dailycaller.com/2026/09/23/diesel-record-refining-margins-crack-spread-export-ban-chris-wright/)). The framework therefore assumes diesel stays at A$3 to 4+/L for at least 12 to 24 months, with physical shortage risk on top of price.

Several timeline sources are aggregators or wiki pages and some disagree on transit counts; treat exact numbers as indicative.

## Diesel supply and demand map

Australia burns about 33 GL of diesel a year, roughly 93 ML a day, and imports about 87% of it. Half of that demand is mining and heavy trucks, so those two uses decide whether net imports can approach zero.

**Supply.** Only two refineries remain: Ampol Lytton (109 kb/d) and Viva Geelong (120 kb/d), which together supply about 13% of diesel ([IEEFA](https://ieefa.org/resources/managing-australias-diesel-squeeze); [EIA](https://www.eia.gov/international/content/analysis/countries_long/Australia/australia.pdf)). Imports came mainly from South Korea (about 29%), Singapore (15%), Malaysia (14%), Brunei (12%), India (9%), Japan (7%) and Taiwan (4%) ([NRMA, Mar 2026](https://www.mynrma.com.au/open-road/news/2026/australia-fuel-supply-chain-facts)). Since March, US and UK cargoes have risen to about 9% of supply ([Crude Oil Peak](https://crudeoilpeak.info/australian-diesel-imports-to-july-2026)), which is exactly the flow a US export ban would cut.

**Stocks.** Diesel stock was 2,938 ML, or 32 days, on 22 September ([fuelplan.gov.au](https://fuelplan.gov.au/fuel-statistics)). The minimum stockholding obligation was cut 20% in March to about 2,200 ML, and that relief now runs to 31 January 2027 ([C-Store](https://www.c-store.com.au/government-extends-diesel-and-petrol-reserve-flexibility/)). Australia remains the only IEA member below the 90-day net import obligation, and has been since 2012 ([SBS](https://www.sbs.com.au/news/article/the-90-day-question-looming-over-australias-fuel-price-pain/zjxno4pbs)).

**Prices.** Average capital-city retail diesel went from 177 c/L before the war (20 Feb) to a peak of 322 c/L (31 Mar), and was 287 c/L on 23 September ([ACCC, Apr](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/weekly-fuel-price-monitoring-report-24-april-2026.pdf); [IndexBox on ACCC, Sep](https://www.indexbox.io/blog/australian-fuel-prices-rise-as-middle-east-conflict-keeps-international-benchmarks-high/)).

*Chart (RenewEconomy 2025 estimates (on-road splits from ABS 2020 survey) · 10 sectors): see the [live doc](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/4ef286f2-95fd-42d2-a0f1-b4cce22dff7a) or the [framework page](index.html).*

**Demand.** The sector split comes from [RenewEconomy](https://reneweconomy.com.au/diesel-replacement-australias-billion-dollar-opportunity-to-electrify-cut-global-fossil-ties/). The official 2023-24 energy statistics put total use at 32.6 GL and mining lower, at 7.8 GL or 24% ([Australia Institute, citing AES Table F](https://australiainstitute.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/P2024-Diesel-use-in-Australian-mining-Web.pdf)). Light vehicles (utes, cars and vans) use about 8 GL, a quarter of the total. Rail, off-grid power and marine are not separately reported in the sources found and sit inside Other.

**Fuel Tax Credits.** Businesses claim more than A$10 bn a year, and mining takes about 47% of it ([The Driven](https://thedriven.io/2026/07/24/a-mining-campaign-dressed-in-an-akubra-the-hands-off-our-fuel-petition-is-not-about-farmers/)). The scheme rebates excise on off-road diesel, so it currently pays heavy users to stay on diesel. It is the single largest fiscal lever in this framework.

## Supply lever: halophyte fuel on saline land

Halophytes are a food-neutral sovereign feedstock, but at realistic yields they replace roughly 1 to 3% of diesel within a decade, not the 25 diesel-days the ENGG1810 report projects. They are worth doing as one stacked land use, with fuel as a co-product, while canola that is currently exported does the heavy lifting on biofuel in the short term.

**What the earlier work found.** The [ENGG1810 Group 365 report](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1OzIg9ydurWmMWaQ1Ar0T_ARysrVcPSOl/view) ranked 12 species with a linear programme. *Atriplex nummularia* (old man saltbush) was the lowest-cost option at about A$480/ha/yr and 220 L/ha. *Salicornia fruticosa* was the knee point at about A$2,200/ha and 580 L/ha. *S. bigelovii* gave the highest yield, 730 L/ha, at about A$3,200/ha because it needs seawater irrigation. Your [Marginal Land Restoration doc](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/74b98fa2-c6b7-4332-9f43-388494bc5700) adds the staging model: stabilise, remediate, then produce, with every site earning from at least three streams.

**Correction to carry forward.** The report's 25 diesel-days at 25% deployment does not reconcile with its own yields. At 25% of 5.7 Mha (1.43 Mha) and about 600 L/ha, output is about 0.85 GL/yr. Against roughly 30 GL/yr of diesel use ([Rabobank, Apr 2026](https://www.rabobank.com.au/news/media-releases/2026/canola-poised-for-expanded-role-in-australias-biofuel-future)), that is about 3% of demand, or roughly 10 diesel-days. Two further cautions apply. The 5.7 Mha figure is the national at-risk projection; land actually salt-affected today is closer to 2 Mha, with 1 to 2 Mha in WA ([WA Auditor General](https://audit.wa.gov.au/reports-and-publications/reports/management-of-salinity/key-findings/dryland-salinity-is-a-significant-cost-and-major-risk-to-the-state/)). And Salicornia's high yields depend on seawater irrigation, so they apply only to coastal flats, not to inland scalds.

| Deployment case | Area | Species and setting | Oil yield (L/ha/yr) | Output (GL/yr) | Share of \~30 GL demand |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Coastal pilot cluster | 50,000 ha | *S. bigelovii* on seawater-irrigated flats | 730 | 0.04 | 0.1% |
| Inland saline programme | 1,000,000 ha | Saltbush and samphire on dryland scalds | 220 | 0.22 | 0.7% |
| Report's 25% case | 1,430,000 ha | Knee-point mix | \~600 | 0.85 | 2.8% |
| Theoretical ceiling | 5,700,000 ha | Knee-point mix | \~600 | 3.4 | 11% |

**Cost per litre.** Using the report's cultivation costs alone, oil costs about A$2.20/L from saltbush, A$3.80/L from *S. fruticosa* and A$4.40/L from *S. bigelovii*, before roughly A$0.30 to 0.60/L for conversion (estimated). That only competes with diesel at crisis prices, or when grazing, carbon credits, Salicornia tips and aquaculture pay most of the land cost, which is the point of the stacked model.

**The faster biofuel lever.** Australia exports about 6 Mt of canola a year, which Rabobank estimates would make more than 2 GL of renewable diesel, about 7% of demand. Up to 90% of the crop goes to European biofuel makers, worth about A$4 bn ([ABC, Apr 2026](https://www.abc.net.au/news/science/2026-04-19/are-saf-biofuels-solution-to-jet-fuel-shortages/106551088)). Tallow and used cooking oil add more. The A$1.1 bn Cleaner Fuels Program is the funding vehicle, with first drop-in fuel expected by 2029, while the companion feedstock strategy prioritises canola, tallow, cane and sorghum over used cooking oil ([S&P Global](https://www.spglobal.com/commodity-insights/en/news-research/latest-news/agriculture/100225-australia-narrows-biofuel-focus-excludes-uco-and-hydrogen-from-key-feedstock-plan)).

**Recommended halophyte programme.**

1. Fund three coastal Salicornia and aquaculture pilots of 1,000 to 5,000 ha on disused salt fields and prawn ponds, where seawater is free.
2. Pay for saltbush on inland scalds mainly through grazing and carbon, and harvest seed oil where yields justify it.
3. Send the oil to FAME at regional crushers first, and to HVO at Kwinana or Geelong when hydrotreating capacity exists.
4. Fund replicated field trials, because no Australian site has yet shown commercial seed-oil yields at scale.

Four of its headline numbers are wrong, though, and three of those overstate the benefit by a factor of 3 to 100. The swapping technology claims mostly check out.

**What carries into this framework.** The agricultural swap standard (AgSwap), the co-op ownership model run through grain handlers such as CBH and GrainCorp, and swap stations on hypersaline land beside wind farms all go into the roadmap below. The WA wheatbelt pilot is resized to a co-op covering about 20,000 ha of cropping. Urea exposure is a separate food-security risk of the same kind as diesel and is added to the roadmap.

## Demand lever: heavy freight, battery swapping and renewable corridors

Electric trucks now pay back in about 2 to 4 years on Australia's busiest corridor, so the task is to remove the upfront cost and charging gaps rather than to prove the technology. Trucks use about 7 GL of diesel a year. Electrifying half of that by 2035 removes about 3.5 GL, roughly 10% of national demand.

**The economics.** An articulated truck averages 78,300 km a year at 53 L/100 km ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/industry/tourism-and-transport/survey-motor-vehicle-use-australia/latest-release)), about 41,600 L of diesel. On the Hume, an electric truck costs about A$0.30/km to run against A$1.03/km for diesel. Electrifying the whole corridor costs about A$4 bn, saves about A$900 m a year in fuel, and pays back in 2 to 4 years ([RenewEconomy, Mar 2026](https://reneweconomy.com.au/electrifying-all-road-freight-between-sydney-and-melbourne-is-a-no-brainer-the-payback-is-less-than-4-years/)). Those figures predate this spring's price rise, so at 280 to 400 c/L the payback is shorter. The barrier is price: a Windrose E700 costs about A$450,000 to 500,000 against about A$250,000 for a diesel prime mover ([Tech Times, Sep 2026](https://www.techtimes.com/articles/327352/20260911/australia-receives-first-sold-electric-semi-trucks-windrose-e700-cuts-costs-84-percent.htm)).

**Why swapping, and its limit.** China sold 231,100 new-energy heavy trucks in 2025, and in December they outsold diesel ([electrive](https://www.electrive.com/2026/01/23/year-end-surge-electric-trucks-outsell-diesel-for-the-first-time-in-china/)). About 30% of electric heavy trucks are swap-capable ([ICCT](https://theicct.org/publication/ze-mhdv-market-in-china-h1-2025-sept25/)). CATL's standard pack fits 95% of mainstream trucks, and its 1,250 km Chengdu to Wuhan route uses 12 stations, about 104 km apart, with 5-minute swaps ([CnEVPost](https://cnevpost.com/2025/05/19/catl-launches-standardized-battery-swap-pack-heavy-trucks/); [CarNewsChina](https://carnewschina.com/2025/12/23/catl-launched-longest-1250-km-heavy-duty-truck-battery-swap-route-in-china/)). The warning from China is that swap stations cost about 2.5 times more than chargers and sit underused when drivers can plug in more cheaply ([36Kr](https://eu.36kr.com/en/p/3473013318506887)). Every hub in this plan should therefore offer both swapping and megawatt charging.

**The Australian swap operator.** Janus Electric converts diesel prime movers to swappable packs that change in about 4 minutes. It had 9 stations, 23 converted trucks and 155 orders by early 2025, including a solar-powered station at Moorebank ([CleanTechnica](https://cleantechnica.com/2025/01/03/janus-electric-prime-movers-a-better-option/)). A conversion costs about A$175,000 against about A$100,000 for a diesel engine rebuild, and Janus plans 50 sites across the national grid ([Proactive Investors](https://www.proactiveinvestors.com.au/companies/news/1089202/janus-electric-flags-five-year-pathway-to-electrify-australia-s-freight-fleet-cutting-diesel-use-by-10bn-litres-1089202.html); [CleanTechnica, Jun 2026](https://cleantechnica.com/2026/06/01/janus-electric-update-talk-to-the-converted/)). Conversion matters in a crisis because it electrifies existing trucks without waiting for new ones.

**Why co-locate with solar and wind farms.** A hub beside a solar farm can charge its spare packs at midday, when wholesale prices are lowest, and hold them for night-time swaps. The stored packs double as grid batteries. Sharing the farm's connection also avoids a separate network connection, which is often the slowest step.

### Incentive package

1. **Hub fund.** Concessional finance from the CEFC and National Reconstruction Fund, plus ARENA grants, for about 30 hubs on the first four corridors. Grants require open access and a common pack standard so any operator can use any hub.
2. **Truck and conversion rebate.** For example, A$100,000 per new electric prime mover and A$75,000 per conversion, means-tested toward owner-drivers and small fleets. At about 41,600 L a year over 10 years, A$100,000 buys out diesel at about A$0.24 per litre avoided (derived).
3. **Turn fuel tax credits into transition credits.** The 2026-27 Budget kept credits for heavy vehicles ([WoodCentral](https://woodcentral.com.au/log-trucks-win-budget-fuel-tax-credits-productivity-commission/)). Let operators take five years of expected credits as an upfront capital grant when they replace a diesel truck, so the subsidy pays for leaving diesel instead of staying on it.
4. **National mass limits.** Adopt NSW's permanent zero-emission concession nationally (8 t steer axle, 18.5 t tandem drive, 26 t gross) so batteries do not cost payload ([Fully Loaded](https://www.fullyloaded.com.au/nsw-zero-emission-truck-trial-set-to-become-permanent)).
5. **Road user charge certainty.** Fix a low, distance-based charge for electric trucks until 2032, as the trucking association proposes, so buyers can price it in ([ATA](https://new.truck.net.au/2026-27-pre-budget-submission/)).
6. **Anchor demand.** Require a rising zero-emission share in federal and state freight contracts, starting with Australia Post, Defence and supermarket supply contracts on the Hume and Pacific.
7. **Scale existing programs.** ARENA's Driving the Nation heavy vehicle round is A$100 m ([DCCEEW](https://minister.dcceew.gov.au/bowen/media-releases/shut-truck-100-million-funding-electrify-australias-trucks-quieter-cleaner-transport)); NSW runs an EV Fleets Incentive for trucks ([Transport for NSW](https://www.transport.nsw.gov.au/operations/freight-hub/towards-net-zero-emissions-freight-policy/knowledge-hub/grants)). Both are an order of magnitude below the A$4 bn the Hume alone needs.

## Demand lever: remote and regional generators

Off-grid power is the cheapest diesel to remove, because solar and batteries already beat diesel generation on cost and new mine hybrids now run at 80 to 96% renewables. The national volume of diesel burned for power is not published in the sources found, so this lever needs an audit before its size can be stated.

**Cost.** Diesel generation costs about A$240 to 300/MWh at large mines and up to A$450/MWh in communities, while remote solar is now below A$100/MWh ([ECD](https://www.ecdonline.com.au/content/efficiency-renewables/article/remote-power-generation-338874428); the diesel figures date from 2013 and are higher at today's prices). States already pay large subsidies to keep remote power affordable: Queensland's community service obligation is A$604 m in FY2026, and WA's Horizon Power receives A$240 to 270 m ([RenewEconomy](https://reneweconomy.com.au/outback-australia-has-desperate-need-for-rooftop-pv-and-batteries-to-cut-crippling-dependence-on-imported-diesel/)). Every litre displaced cuts those subsidies as well as imports.

| Site | System | Renewable share or diesel saved |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mount Weld (Lynas) | Wind, solar and battery hybrid | 95.7% renewable in the March 2026 quarter; 870,000 L less diesel than a year earlier ([pv magazine, Aug 2026](http://www.pv-magazine.com/2026/08/04/remote-operations-emerge-as-proving-ground-for-high-renewables-future/)) |
| Bellevue Gold | 27 MW solar, 24 MW wind, 30 MWh battery | 93.8% renewable in February 2026 (same source) |
| Kathleen Valley (Liontown) | Wind, solar and battery hybrid | 82% renewable (same source) |
| Agnew (Gold Fields) | 18 MW wind, 4 MW solar, 13 MW battery | 50 to 60% renewable ([EDL](https://edlenergy.com/project/agnew/)) |
| Fortescue, Pilbara | 1.2 GW solar, 600+ MW wind, 4 GWh battery by 2028 | Replaces diesel and gas across its network ([The Driven](http://thedriven.io/2026/09/25/the-simple-tricks-fortescue-is-using-to-convert-its-fleet-of-giant-diesel-haul-trucks-to-full-battery-electric/)) |
| 25 NT communities (SETuP) | 10 MW solar | About 15% diesel cut ([One Step Off the Grid](https://onestepoffthegrid.com.au/nt-completes-10mw-solar-roll-out-to-25-remote-indigenous-communities/)) |
| 6 WA towns (Horizon Power) | 2 MW solar, 4.3 MWh battery | Cuts towns' diesel dependence ([pv magazine](https://www.pv-magazine-australia.com/2024/11/13/horizon-power-solar-and-bess-projects-free-remote-towns-of-diesel-dependence/)) |

The lesson from the table is that solar alone cuts about 15%, while solar with enough battery storage cuts 50 to 96%. Programs should fund storage, not just panels.

**Scale of the opportunity.** More than 100 of Australia's roughly 350 mines are off-grid ([Mining Technology](https://www.mining-technology.com/features/off-grid-on-site-power-australian-mines/)), along with 200 to 300 diesel-dependent remote communities. Maningrida alone uses up to 10,000 L a day ([pv magazine, Mar 2026](http://www.pv-magazine-australia.com/2026/03/18/are-microgrids-answer-to-remote-communities-energy-conumdrum/)). Existing programs are small: ARENA's Regional Microgrids Program is A$125 m, A$75 m of it for First Nations communities ([ARENA](https://arena.gov.au/news/arena-backs-first-nations-led-microgrids-to-improve-power-reliability-in-remote-nt-communities/)).

**Recommended program.**

1. **Audit within 3 months.** Require every generator above 1 MW to report its annual diesel use, so the lever can be sized and tracked.
2. **Community hybrid rollout.** Convert all 200 to 300 diesel-dependent communities to at least 70% renewable by 2030, with First Nations ownership options. Finance it from the state power subsidies it reduces.
3. **Mine power contracts.** Let off-grid mines claim fuel tax credits only for diesel above a declining cap per MWh generated, starting in 2028. This pushes every mine toward the 80%+ hybrids already proven.
4. **Keep diesel as backup.** Retain generators for reliability, but run them for hours a year rather than all day. This also stretches reserves during a supply crisis.

## Demand lever: buses, rail, utes and cars

Light vehicles and buses use about 8.6 GL of diesel a year, a quarter of demand. The market is already moving: battery EVs reached a record 20% of new sales in May 2026. Grants should target high-kilometre ute drivers, because a grant per diesel litre avoided costs about four times more for a ute than for a truck.

**Buses.** Buses use about 0.5 GL. NSW runs 8,000+ diesel and gas buses and plans about 1,700 zero-emission buses for Sydney by 2028, with full transition by 2035 in Sydney and 2047 in regional NSW ([Transport for NSW](https://www.transport.nsw.gov.au/projects/current-projects/zero-emission-buses)). Victoria's 4,500 diesel buses can only be replaced with zero-emission buses from 1 July 2025 ([Victorian Government](https://www.vic.gov.au/zero-emissions-bus-trial-and-transition-plan)). Queensland dropped its south-east electric bus commitment on 1 September 2026 and is buying up to 630 buses with no electric models ([The Driven](http://thedriven.io/2026/09/01/fanciful-pipe-dream-queensland-dumps-electric-bus-mandate-describing-them-as-big-expensive-paperweights/)). An electric bus costs about 30% more up front but about A$275,000 less over its life ([CommBank, 2022](https://www.commbank.com.au/business/brighter-perspectives/why-electric-buses-are-the-future-of-public-transport.html)).

**Rail.** NSW's 29 new regional trains are bi-mode diesel and electric, with 16 in testing at Dubbo. Aurizon will trial a 2.3 MWh battery tender on the Kwinana to Pinjarra line with an A$9.4 m ARENA grant ([Railway Gazette, Aug 2026](https://www.railwaygazette.com/battery-traction/2026/08/16/australia-betting-on-batteries-to-decarbonise-heavy-freight/)). Shifting long-haul freight from road to rail saves diesel even before rail is electrified, but rail's diesel use was not in the sources found.

**Utes and cars.** Diesel was 29% of the 1.24 m new vehicles sold in 2025, and the Ford Ranger was the top seller ([CarExpert](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/vfacts-2025-another-record-year-for-new-vehicle-sales-in-australia-but-growth-modest-overall)). In May 2026 battery EVs hit 20% and all electrified vehicles 46% ([carsales](https://business.carsales.com.au/news-room/news/vfacts-may-2026-bevs-record-tesla-tops-the-charts/)). Electrified utes now cost A$57,000 to 83,000 drive-away, from the GWM Cannon Alpha and Ford Ranger PHEVs to the BYD Shark 6 (100 km electric range) and Toyota HiLux BEV ([CarExpert](https://www.carexpert.com.au/car-news/every-electrified-ute-available-in-australia-in-2026)). The EV fringe benefits tax exemption runs in full until 31 March 2027, but plug-in hybrids lost it for new leases in April 2025 ([Hudson](https://hudsonfinancialplanning.com.au/resources/education-reports/fbt-electric-vehicle-exemption-australia/)).

**Home energy is the enabler.** The Cheaper Home Batteries program passed 507,000 installs (about 14 GWh) by August 2026 and its budget rose to A$7.2 bn ([Energy Storage News](https://www.energy-storage.news/australias-cheaper-home-batteries-program-officially-passes-500000-installations/)). A household with rooftop solar and a battery can charge a vehicle mostly from its own roof, which makes the vehicle grant go further.

**Grant value per litre (derived).** About 2.9 m diesel dual-cab utes use about 4.4 GL a year, or about 1,500 L each ([Australia Institute](https://australiainstitute.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/P2024-Diesel-use-in-Australian-mining-Web.pdf)). A A$15,000 grant for a battery ute that replaces 1,500 L a year for 10 years costs about A$1.00 per litre avoided. A A$10,000 plug-in hybrid grant, assuming 60% electric driving, costs about A$1.10 per litre. The truck rebate above costs about A$0.24 per litre.

**Recommended measures.**

1. **Ute and car swap grant.** A$15,000 for a battery ute and A$10,000 for a plug-in hybrid ute when a diesel vehicle is retired, limited to drivers who log more than 25,000 km a year, such as farmers, tradespeople and regional fleets.
2. **Restore and extend fringe benefits tax relief.** Return plug-in hybrid utes to the exemption until 2030, and keep the full exemption for battery EVs past March 2027.
3. **Bundle with home batteries.** Offer an extra A$2,000 to households that add a bidirectional charger alongside a rebated home battery.
4. **Fund the bus transition.** A federal fund matching state spending on electric buses, with Queensland eligible if it returns to electric purchasing.
5. **Emergency demand cuts.** In the crisis phase, halve public transport fares and fund remote-work days for public servants, the demand-restraint steps the national fuel plan already contemplates before rationing.

## Demand lever: mining and agriculture

Mining is the largest single user at 7.8 to 9.6 GL a year, and 15 companies account for 77% of it. Fortescue says battery haul trucks have reached cost parity and will replace all its diesel haul trucks by 2030, so the policy job is to make every large miner follow on that timeline. Agriculture uses about 2.3 GL and is the sector where shortages translate directly into food supply risk.

**Mining.** Mining's diesel use has tripled since 2006-07, and the 15 largest coal and iron ore producers use 77% of it ([Australia Institute](https://australiainstitute.org.au/report/diesel-use-in-australian-mining/)). Fortescue's first Liebherr battery haul truck arrives in late 2026, with 6 MW chargers and a target of no diesel haul trucks by 2030 ([The Driven, Sep 2026](http://thedriven.io/2026/09/25/the-simple-tricks-fortescue-is-using-to-convert-its-fleet-of-giant-diesel-haul-trucks-to-full-battery-electric/)). BHP and Rio Tinto have run two Caterpillar battery haul trucks at Jimblebar since late 2025, with in-motion charging trials next ([BHP, Jun 2026](https://www.bhp.com/news/media-centre/releases/2026/06/bhp-rio-tinto-and-caterpillar-launch-battery-electric-haul-truck-trial-in-the-pilbara)). Mines also burn diesel for power, covered in the remote generation section.

**Agriculture.** Diesel use per hectare ranges from 23 L for sorghum to 64 L for conventionally tilled wheat. Tillage can be up to 66% of a grain farm's fuel and pumping up to 51% on irrigated farms ([NSW DPI](https://www.dpi.nsw.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0011/1321796/mov3ment-diesel-use-in-ag.pdf)). Pumps are the easy win: a Darling Downs farm that put 25 kW of solar on an 18 kW bore pump saves A$8,000 a year ([CommBank, Apr 2026](https://www.commbank.com.au/articles/newsroom/2026/04/is-there-a-case-for-electrification-in-australian-farming.html)). No-till cuts tillage diesel roughly in half. Harvest and large tractors are the hard part, which is where shared swap stations from the report above fit.

**Recommended measures.**

1. **Mining electrification compact.** Require the 15 largest diesel-using miners to publish fleet electrification plans with dated milestones, as a condition of continued full fuel tax credits after 2030.
2. **Fuel tax credit capital conversion.** Let miners and farmers convert future credits into upfront grants for electric equipment, as proposed for trucks.
3. **Solar pumping rebate.** A 50% rebate on solar and battery systems that replace diesel pumps, available immediately through farm businesses.
4. **No-till and controlled traffic support.** Extension funding through GRDC, since practice change is the fastest way to cut tillage diesel.
5. **Ag swap co-op pilot.** One WA wheatbelt co-op of about 20,000 ha, sharing a wind-and-solar swap station for harvest machinery, run through a grain handler.
6. **Farm fuel priority.** In the crisis phase, reserve a share of diesel stock for harvest, sowing and food freight under the national fuel plan, and add urea to stockholding planning.

## Integrated economics

The combined package cuts net diesel imports from about 29 GL to about 15.5 GL a year by 2035, saving roughly A$17 bn to 28 bn a year in import costs at 2026 prices. Getting close to zero takes until about 2045 and needs mining and heavy freight almost fully electric. The 10-year public cost is about what fuel tax credits cost in 18 months.

*Chart (Lever estimates in this doc · baseline 33.5 GL demand, 87% imported): see the [live doc](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/4ef286f2-95fd-42d2-a0f1-b4cce22dff7a) or the [framework page](index.html).*

**How the steps were sized (estimates).** Mining: about 40% of 9.6 GL by 2035, led by Fortescue's 2030 target. Trucks: half of about 7 GL. Light vehicles: about 40% of 8 GL as electric sales compound through fleet turnover. Renewable diesel: about 2 GL from canola now exported, plus tallow and a small halophyte share. Remote power and agriculture are deliberately conservative until the generator audit is done. Domestic refining is assumed to keep running.

**Import bill.** At Singapore gasoil prices between about 126 c/L (June) and 209 c/L (March peak) ([ACCC via IndexBox](https://www.indexbox.io/blog/australian-fuel-prices-rise-as-middle-east-conflict-keeps-international-benchmarks-high/); [ACCC](https://www.accc.gov.au/system/files/weekly-fuel-price-monitoring-report-24-april-2026.pdf)), today's 29 GL of imports costs about A$37 bn to 61 bn a year. Removing 13.6 GL saves about A$17 bn to 28 bn a year (derived).

| Lever | Public cost per litre of imports avoided | Indicative 10-year public cost | Payback for the user |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Remote and mine power hybrids | Negative: power gets cheaper | Financed from state power subsidies | Under 5 years at A$240 to 450/MWh diesel power |
| Mining haul trucks | Near zero: cost parity claimed | Credit conversion only | At parity now (Fortescue) |
| Electric trucks and conversions | About A$0.24 (derived) | About A$4 bn for 40,000 trucks, plus about A$2 bn of concessional hub finance | 2 to 4 years on the Hume |
| Utes and cars, targeted | About A$1.00 to 1.10 (derived) | About A$4 bn for 300,000 vehicles | Fuel savings plus grant |
| Electric buses | Low: lifetime cost already lower | About A$1 bn matched with states | Over bus life |
| Canola and halophyte renewable diesel | Premium over fossil diesel; Australian sustainable aviation fuel costs about A$2.80/L more than jet fuel ([ABC](https://www.abc.net.au/news/science/2026-04-19/are-saf-biofuels-solution-to-jet-fuel-shortages/106551088)) | Existing A$1.1 bn Cleaner Fuels Program, expanded | Depends on a production credit |

**Total.** About A$12 bn to 15 bn of public money over 10 years, against fuel tax credits of more than A$10 bn every year. Electrification also cuts the cost of the next shock: every litre not imported is a litre that does not need to be stockpiled under the 90-day obligation.

**Sensitivity.** If diesel falls back to pre-war prices (about 177 c/L retail), paybacks lengthen by roughly a third but every lever except the biofuel ones still pays back within the life of the equipment. If the US bans exports and prices pass A$4/L, paybacks shorten by about a third and the case for the emergency measures becomes overwhelming.

## Roadmap and policy package

The first 12 months protect food supply and buy time; the next three years build the corridors and supply chains; 2030 to 2035 scales them. Each phase ends at a gate the government can measure.

*Chart (roadmap · 3 phases, 3 gates): see the [live doc](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/4ef286f2-95fd-42d2-a0f1-b4cce22dff7a) or the [framework page](index.html).*

**Phase 1: Emergency (October 2026 to September 2027).** The aim is to keep harvest, sowing and food freight moving if a US export ban lands.

1. **Ring-fence food diesel.** Pre-agree under the national fuel plan that farms, food processors and food freight get priority supply at the voluntary-restraint stage (15 days of cover), not only at rationing (10 days).
2. **Revive the fuel task force** and publish weekly stock data by region, so shortages are seen before stations run dry.
3. **Demand restraint first.** Half-price public transport, remote-work days and freight consolidation, triggered before rationing.
4. **Diversify supply now.** Seek term diesel contracts with Korean, Singaporean, Malaysian, Japanese and Indian refiners to replace US cargoes, and keep the stockholding relief tied to a refill plan.
5. **Add urea to strategic stocks**, since there is no urea reserve and 65 to 70% comes from the Gulf.
6. **Fast-track the quick wins:** truck conversions, solar pumps and remote hybrids, which can be installed within a season.
7. **Keep canola onshore.** Offer crush and offtake contracts so part of the harvest is turned into domestic biodiesel instead of exported.

**Phase 2: Build (2027 to 2030).** Launch the corridor hub fund, the truck rebate and fuel tax credit conversion, the community hybrid rollout, the mining compact, the first renewable diesel plant under the Cleaner Fuels Program, the coastal halophyte pilots and the WA AgSwap co-op. Restore fringe benefits tax relief for plug-in hybrid utes and fund electric buses with the states.

**Phase 3: Scale (2030 to 2035).** Extend hubs to the Nullarbor, Stuart and Great Northern corridors with stand-alone microgrids. Convert fuel tax credits into transition credits. Scale halophyte production only where trials meet cost and yield targets.

**Who leads.** DCCEEW (fuel security, stockholding), Infrastructure (corridors, road user charge, mass limits through the NTC and NHVR), Treasury (fuel tax credits), ARENA and CEFC (finance), state energy agencies (hubs, renewable zones, buses), and grain handlers and Traditional Owner groups (co-ops and land partnerships).

## Risks, open questions and sources

**Main risks.**

- **The crisis ends early.** If Hormuz reopens and prices fall, political support fades before the build phase. The gates should be legislated so funding does not depend on the price of the week.
- **Swap stations sit idle.** China's experience shows drivers bypass swapping when plug-in charging is cheaper. Hubs must offer both, and a common pack standard must be set before money is spent.
- **Grid connection delays.** Hubs beside solar and wind farms reduce this risk but do not remove it.
- **Biofuel feedstock competition.** Canola diverted to renewable diesel is lost export revenue, and halophyte yields are unproven in Australia. Biofuel is sized as a supplement, not the main lever.
- **Critical minerals and battery supply.** Packs and trucks are imported, largely from China, which swaps one dependency for another. It is a smaller risk, because a battery delivered once keeps working, while diesel must be imported continuously.
- **Equity.** Rebates favour those who can buy new vehicles. Means-testing, conversions and second-hand eligibility matter for owner-drivers and farmers.

**Open questions.**

- [ ] How much diesel is burned for electricity, by site? This needs the generator audit.
- [ ] What do rail and marine use? These were not in the sources found.
- [ ] Was the stockholding relief extended past 30 September, and is the fuel excise cut still in force?
- [ ] What do halophytes actually yield on Australian saline sites? This needs replicated field trials.
- [ ] What pack size and mass work for combines and large tractors?
